# A brief history of bank notes in the United States and some lessons for stablecoins

## Citation

Carlson, Mark. "A brief history of bank notes in the United States and some lessons for stablecoins". FEDS Notes, Federal Reserve Board, 6 February 2026. https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/a-brief-history-of-bank-notes-in-the-united-states-and-some-lessons-for-stablecoins-20260206.html.

## One-line summary

A Federal Reserve historian draws on the pre-1913 bank note era to argue that redemption access, not collateral quality alone, determines whether privately issued money trades at par.

## Key claims

- Before the Federal Reserve, commercial banks issued privately issued bank notes secured by collateral such as state or federal bonds.
- During the Free Banking era, notes redeemed away from the issuing bank traded at discounts, imposing transaction costs on merchants who had to track inter-bank exchange rates.
- A 1874 Treasury change let holders redeem national bank notes at any sub-Treasury office nationwide, and this access made all notes trade uniformly at par.
- Uniform par trading made national bank notes "informationally insensitive": holders no longer needed to track which bank had issued a given note.
- Stablecoin holders typically cannot redeem directly with the issuer and instead rely on authorised redemption agents, recreating the Free Banking era's access friction.
- Research cited in the note finds that a wider network of redemption agents narrows the deviation of stablecoin prices from par.
- The historical model differs from the GENIUS Act framework because 19th century note-issuing banks held no other liabilities, while stablecoin issuers today hold only stablecoin liabilities on a narrower balance sheet.

## Significance

- Supplies the wiki with a Federal Reserve staff historical analogy that treats redemption network breadth, not collateral quality alone, as the determinant of stablecoin par stability.
- Anchors the "informational insensitivity" concept from Gorton (2010) to the stablecoin design debate, a term likely to recur across the stablecoins domain.
- Identifies a structural gap between the historical national bank note regime and the GENIUS Act model: issuers today hold only stablecoin liabilities, with no cross-subsidising deposit book.
- Gives the wiki a primary source for redemption-agent design questions that sit upstream of any GENIUS Act reserve or disclosure rule.
