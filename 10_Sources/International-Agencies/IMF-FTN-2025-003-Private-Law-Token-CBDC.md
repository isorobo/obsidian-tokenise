# Private Law Aspects of Token-Based Central Bank Digital Currencies

## Citation

Bechara, Marianne, Wouter Bossu, Amira Rasekh, Chia Yi Tan, and Akihiro Yoshinaga. "Private Law Aspects of Token-Based Central Bank Digital Currencies." IMF FinTech Notes, NOTE/2025/003, 14 March 2025. https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fintech-notes/issues/2025/03/14/private-law-aspects-of-token-based-central-bank-digital-currencies-565165.

## One-line summary

The note provides a cross-jurisdictional private law framework for retail token-based CBDC, addressing property classification, ownership evidence, transfer mechanics, custody, pledging, and choice-of-law rules.

## Key claims

- Token-based CBDC constitutes a legally novel form of money: the monetary claim on the central bank is incorporated in a digital token, and transfer of the token equals transfer of the claim, without any current-account relationship between the central bank and the holder.
- Existing private law categories (tangible property, intangible property, hybrid assets) do not map cleanly onto token-based CBDC; legislatures may need to create a new sui generis category or adapt existing frameworks.
- Ownership evidence depends on the ledger and wallet design; intermediated holding structures require law to guarantee that CBDC holders always hold a direct claim on the central bank even if an intermediary becomes insolvent.
- The transfer of token-based CBDC raises nemo dat questions: acquiring a token in good faith may or may not extinguish prior claims depending on how domestic law treats bearer instruments and digital assets.
- Private international law rules for token-based CBDC remain unsettled; the note proposes a regime that attaches the governing law to the jurisdiction of the CBDC payment platform, not the location of the holder.
- Law reform is necessary in most jurisdictions: existing property, insolvency, and conflict-of-law rules were not designed for a direct-claim digital bearer instrument issued by a central bank.

## Excerpts

> "In contrast to account-based CBDC, token-based CBDC would legally truly represent a 'new form of money.'"
> ~ §II, p. 4

> "For a token-based CBDC to complement successfully other forms of money and circulate widely by being used daily by the general public, it must interact well with private law mechanisms that underpin its circulation and usage by economic agents. These mechanisms pertain for instance to the transfer, collateralization, and custody of the CBDC. Legal uncertainty in these areas could hinder the wide adoption of CBDCs."
> ~ §I, p. 2

> "The law must ensure that holders always have a direct claim on the central bank and are protected if the intermediary becomes insolvent. Without these safeguards, token-based CBDCs would not be risk-free central bank money, raising questions about its role in the monetary system."
> ~ §I, p. 2

> "The incorporation of monetary (and other) claims in an immaterial, digital token is quite unprecedented. At any rate, the legal concept of token must be distinguished from the technological concept."
> ~ §II, p. 4

## Significance

- Establishes the only IMF-issued analytical framework for the private law design of retail token-based CBDC, directly shaping national law reform programmes.
- The custody and insolvency analysis applies equally to tokenised deposits and tokenised securities held through intermediaries, extending the note's relevance beyond CBDC.
- The choice-of-law proposals feed directly into UNIDROIT's Digital Assets Working Group and the broader international harmonisation agenda on digital-asset private law.
- The property-classification analysis anchors the `property-category` doctrine thread in this vault and connects the CBDC design debate to the RWA tokenisation corpus.
