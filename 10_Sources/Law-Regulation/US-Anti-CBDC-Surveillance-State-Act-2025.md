# Anti-CBDC Surveillance State Act: Federal Reserve CBDC Prohibition

## Citation

United States Congress, House Bill 1919 (119th Congress). "Anti-CBDC Surveillance State Act." Introduced February 2025 by Representative Tom Emmer (R-MN); companion bill S.1124 introduced by Senator Ted Cruz (R-TX). House passed July 2, 2025 (219–210). https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1919

## Legislative Timeline

- **Introduced (House)**: February 2025 (H.R. 1919) by Rep. Tom Emmer
- **Introduced (Senate)**: February 2025 (S.1124) by Sen. Ted Cruz
- **House Rules Committee approved**: June 2025
- **House passed**: July 2, 2025 (219–210 party-line vote)
- **Senate status**: Pending (as of mid-2026)
- **Executive Order** (related): Trump Executive Order banning federal CBDC exploration (2025)

## One-line Summary

Legislation prohibiting the Federal Reserve from issuing, testing, developing, or implementing any central bank digital currency (CBDC) for retail or wholesale use, and barring the Fed from maintaining accounts on behalf of individuals, codifying a statutory ban on Fed CBDC issuance without explicit Congressional authorisation.

---

## Core Prohibitions

The Act imposes four statutory restrictions on the Federal Reserve:

### Prohibition 1: Direct CBDC Issuance
**Text**: "No Federal Reserve Bank may issue a central bank digital currency or any digital asset that is substantially similar to a central bank digital currency under any name or label."

**Effect**: Prohibits Fed-issued CBDC for any purpose (retail, wholesale, experimental, or operational).

**Scope**: Covers not just CBDCs but any "substantially similar" digital asset, preventing regulatory workaround through relabeling.

### Prohibition 2: Indirect CBDC Issuance
**Text**: "No Federal Reserve Bank may indirectly issue a central bank digital currency through any financial institution or other intermediary."

**Effect**: Even if a Fed-issued CBDC were distributed through commercial banks or fintechs as intermediaries, such distribution is prohibited.

**Scope**: Closes the two-tier CBDC model (Fed issues, banks distribute) that central banks worldwide use for retail CBDC.

**Implication**: Cannot establish a wholesale CBDC for settlement purposes if retail access is contemplated, because intermediary distribution is barred.

### Prohibition 3: Testing, Development, and Implementation
**Text**: "The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System may not test, study, develop, create, or implement a central bank digital currency, or any digital asset that is substantially similar to a central bank digital currency."

**Effect**: Prohibits R&D, pilots, pilots-of-pilots, and forward-looking CBDC preparedness.

**Scope**: Prevents Fed staff from:
- Conducting CBDC design studies
- Running technological proofs-of-concept
- Drafting CBDC implementation frameworks
- Participating in international CBDC coordination (e.g., Project Agorá)

**Current impact**: Freezes ongoing Fed CBDC research initiated under prior administrations.

### Prohibition 4: CBDC-Based Monetary Policy
**Text**: "The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve Committee may not use a central bank digital currency to implement monetary policy."

**Effect**: Even if a CBDC somehow existed, the Fed cannot use it as a policy tool.

**Scope**: Bars programmable money, velocity controls, expiration dates, and negative interest rates implemented through CBDC infrastructure.

---

## Statutory Requirements

### Congressional Authorisation Prerequisite
The Act establishes that **no CBDC can be issued without explicit Congressional authorisation**. Current law (Federal Reserve Act, Monetary Control Act) does not authorize CBDC issuance; separate legislation would be required to reverse this prohibition.

### No Workaround Authority
The Act clarifies that:
- Executive orders cannot override the statutory ban
- Federal Reserve policy statements do not constitute authority to issue CBDC
- International agreements do not authorize CBDC issuance
- Regulatory guidance does not circumvent the prohibition

## Policy Rationale and Arguments

### Stated Concerns in Legislative Record

The bill is grounded in four primary concerns, reflected in sponsor statements and House Financial Services Committee testimony:

#### 1. Financial Privacy and Fourth Amendment Rights
**Argument**: A Fed CBDC would create a digital currency system enabling transaction-level surveillance by the central bank.

**Risk articulation**: Central bank would have real-time visibility into all financial transactions of all citizens, enabling:
- Political targeting (withholding digital currency from dissidents)
- Tax enforcement at unprecedented precision
- Control over economic activity (disabling currency during protests)
- Weaponisation of monetary policy against political opponents

**Libertarian position**: Violates Fourth Amendment privacy protections and erodes financial autonomy.

#### 2. Programmable Money and Authoritarian Control
**Argument**: CBDC enables central-bank-controlled monetary policy incompatible with a free society.

**Technical concern**: Unlike cash or current bank deposits, a CBDC could be programmed to:
- Expire after fixed periods (forcing spending)
- Restrict purchasing categories (ban ammunition, energy, travel)
- Implement negative interest rates automatically (confiscatory policy)
- Be frozen unilaterally by the Fed without due process

**Risk**: CBDC becomes a control mechanism rather than a neutral medium of exchange.

#### 3. International Competitiveness
**Paradox**: While framing CBDC as surveillance risk, the bill also argues that **other nations (China, EU, Singapore) are building CBDCs that will displace the dollar**. U.S. refusal to build CBDC may cede global monetary leadership.

**Resolving tension**: The bill prioritises privacy over monetary dominance—arguing that a surveillance-based CBDC is not worth global leadership.

#### 4. Banking Disintermediation Risk
**Argument** (aligned with BIS research): A retail CBDC would trigger deposit outflows from banks during crises, destabilising the financial system.

**Risk mechanism**: Households would move savings from bank deposits to risk-free Fed CBDC, especially during financial stress, accelerating bank runs.

**Solution**: Statutory ban on retail CBDC preserves the banking system's funding base.

## Relationship to GENIUS Act (Simultaneous Legislation)

The Anti-CBDC Act and GENIUS Act were passed **simultaneously in July 2025** and represent **opposing regulatory philosophies**:

| Aspect | Anti-CBDC Act | GENIUS Act |
|--------|---------------|-----------|
| **Central authority** | Federal Reserve | Private stablecoin issuers (banks, fintechs) |
| **Digital currency form** | Prohibited (CBDC) | Permitted (payment stablecoins) |
| **Reserve backing** | N/A (no CBDC allowed) | Required (1:1 backing) |
| **Surveillance risk** | High (motivates ban) | Mitigated by private competition |
| **Banking intermediation** | Preserved (bank deposits remain) | Transformed (bank-issued stablecoins + private competitors) |

**Implication**: U.S. monetary system will evolve through **private stablecoins + banking deposits**, NOT through Fed CBDC.

## What the Act Permits (Wholesale CBDC via Project Agorá)

### Strategic Ambiguity

The Anti-CBDC Act prohibits **retail** CBDC and testing/development generally. However, legal scholars and Fed officials interpret the Act as potentially permitting **wholesale CBDC** under narrow conditions:

**Permitted interpretation**:
- Wholesale CBDC issued to **banks only** (not individuals) may not trigger "indirect issuance" because banks are not end-user "individuals"
- If a Fed wholesale CBDC were **already authorised under separate law** (e.g., by Congress in a future statute), the prohibition on "testing, developing, creating" would no longer apply

**Prohibited interpretation**:
- Cannot test or develop wholesale CBDC pilots now (Prohibition 3 applies)
- Cannot use any CBDC for monetary policy (Prohibition 4 applies)
- Cannot distribute to banks with intent that banks distribute to individuals (Prohibition 2 applies)

**Practical effect**: Wholesale CBDC for interbank settlement is theoretically possible only if:
1. Congress explicitly authorises it in separate legislation
2. Fed demonstrates it serves no retail purpose
3. Fed uses it for operational settlement only, not monetary policy

## International Context: The CBDC Divergence

As of mid-2026, the global CBDC landscape shows sharp divergence:

| Region | CBDC Status | Rationale |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| **China** | mCBDC (Digital Currency Electronic Payment) live; programmable money enabled | Monetary control + capital controls + financial surveillance |
| **EU** | eEURO pilot underway; multi-central-bank wholesale CBDC (Project Agorá) in development | Preserve monetary sovereignty; compete with dollar; interoperability |
| **Singapore/Hong Kong** | Wholesale CBDC pilots (Project Agorá, EnsembleTX); retail CBDC excluded | Settlement efficiency; institutional focus; avoid retail disruption |
| **UK** | Britcoin digital pound in design phase | Monetary modernisation; maintain GBP competitiveness |
| **USA** | Anti-CBDC statutory ban (this Act); private stablecoins permitted (GENIUS Act) | Privacy-first; banking-system preservation; private-sector innovation |

**Strategic question addressed by Columbia Law CLS Blue Sky blog**: Does U.S. refusal to build CBDC diminish American influence over evolving global monetary standards, allowing foreign platforms and currencies to displace the dollar?

## Statutory Scope: What Is Prohibited?

### Explicitly Prohibited Under the Act
1. ✅ **Fed-issued retail CBDC** for households
2. ✅ **Fed-issued wholesale CBDC** for banks (during prohibition period; would require separate authorisation)
3. ✅ **Programmable money** using CBDC infrastructure
4. ✅ **Pilot programs** testing CBDC technology
5. ✅ **Negative interest rates** implemented via CBDC
6. ✅ **Two-tier CBDC** (Fed issues, banks distribute)
7. ✅ **CBDC-based monetary policy** (velocity controls, expiration, etc.)

### Explicitly Permitted Under the Act
1. ✅ **Private stablecoins** (USDT, USDC, JPM Coin, etc.) — separate GENIUS Act regime applies
2. ✅ **Bank deposits** as store of value
3. ✅ **Fed reserve deposits** for banks (existing system)
4. ✅ **Tokenised federal reserves** (if issued by non-Fed entities)
5. ✅ **Cash** in circulation
6. ✅ **Wholesale CBDC** if authorised by future Congressional legislation (explicit statutory language required)

## Repeal and Modification

### How to Reverse the Ban

Since the Act is statutory (not just Fed guidance), reversing it requires:
1. New Act of Congress explicitly authorising CBDC
2. Signed by President
3. Supersedes Anti-CBDC Act via **later-in-time rule** of statutory interpretation

**Political likelihood (as of mid-2026)**: Low in near term; requires change in Congressional control and administration position.

### Proposed Amendments
- Some economic analysis centres argue for narrower ban (retail only; permit wholesale)
- Fed staff papers discuss wish for regulatory flexibility
- None have advanced legislatively (as of June 2026)

## Implications for U.S. Monetary System

### Settlement Layer Evolution (2026–2030)

**Pre-Anti-CBDC scenario** (speculated before Act):
- Fed issues wholesale CBDC to banks → banks settle intraday transactions on Fed ledger → retail CBDC follows for consumer payments

**Post-Anti-CBDC scenario** (actual path):
- Fed continues to issue reserve deposits (existing system)
- Private banks issue stablecoins (JPM Coin, bank-issued tokenised deposits)
- Private stablecoins settle on permissioned ledgers or public blockchains (Ethereum, Solana)
- Fed remains "backstop" via discount window but does not compete with private stablecoins

**Implication**: U.S. monetary system becomes **tiered**:
- Tier 1: Fed reserve deposits (banks only)
- Tier 2: Bank-issued stablecoins and tokenised deposits (institutional and retail)
- Tier 3: Private stablecoins (USDC, USDT, others) (retail and institutional)
- Tier 4: Non-compliant stablecoins (regulatory arbitrage jurisdictions)

### Impact on Cross-Border Settlement

**Without Fed CBDC**, cross-border settlement reverts to:
- Traditional correspondent banking (slow, expensive)
- Private stablecoin bridges (USDC, JPM Coin on Ethereum or permissioned networks)
- Foreign CBDCs (eEURO, Digital Yuan) for non-dollar trading
- Swift interconnection with tokenised settlement networks

**Result**: Fed influence over international settlement standards may decline; European and Chinese digital currencies gain relative prominence.

| Jurisdiction | Approach | CBDC Status | Privacy Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USA** | Statutory ban on CBDC | Prohibited (Anti-CBDC Act) | Prevents government surveillance infrastructure |
| **Switzerland** | Privacy-first design | Retail CBDC under study; privacy by design mandated | Anonymity provisions; offline capability |
| **ECB/EU** | Privacy + innovation balance | eEURO with privacy protections (pseudonymity for transactions <EUR 3,000) | Limited anonymity; KYC required above threshold |
| **China** | Programmable CBDC | mCBDC (Digital Yuan) live; programmable money enabled | **No privacy**: designed for monetary control and surveillance |
| **Singapore** | Institutional focus | Wholesale CBDC only; retail excluded | Privacy preserved via wholesale-only architecture |

**U.S. position**: Most privacy-protective (statutory ban); trades potential monetary dominance for financial privacy.

## Significance

- **Historic**: First nation-state to **statutorily prohibit** its own central bank from issuing CBDC
- **Privacy sentinel**: Establishes U.S. law as protective of financial privacy via CBDC prohibition (vs. surveillance states building programmable money)
- **Libertarian victory**: Reflects successful libertarian and conservative opposition to CBDC, marking retreat from earlier Fed enthusiasm (2019–2024)
- **Banking system preservation**: Statutory protection of deposit-based intermediation; prevents disintermediation via retail CBDC
- **International dynamics**: Creates divergence with EU (eEURO in development) and Asia (wholesale CBDCs in pilots); may reduce Fed influence over global monetary standards
- **Stablecoin implication**: Makes private stablecoins and bank-issued tokens the primary innovation pathway for digital money (vs. central-bank-led CBDC)
