# Towards a theory of shadow money

## Citation

Gabor, Daniela, and Jakob Vestergaard. "Towards a theory of shadow money". INET Working Paper, April 2016 (the text proxy dated the PDF 14 April 2016). https://www.ineteconomics.org/uploads/papers/Towards_Theory_Shadow_Money_GV_INET.pdf.

## One-line summary

Gabor and Vestergaard define shadow money as repo liabilities backed by tradable collateral and argue that its par convertibility rests on collateral market liquidity, so central banks and treasuries must stabilise markets, not only institutions.

## Key claims

- Money is a hierarchy of provisional promises to pay, and a promise is credible only if it converts at par into money higher in the hierarchy.
- Shadow money is defined narrowly as repo liabilities supported by tradable collateral, which separates it from asset-backed commercial paper and money market fund shares.
- Repo-based shadow money is more fragile than bank money because its convertibility depends on collateral market liquidity rather than explicit or implicit public guarantees.
- Banks, and not only non-bank shadow lenders, issue shadow money, including in bank-based systems such as the euro area and China.
- Government bonds are the base asset of shadow money, so sovereign debt issuance and collateral policy shape how much shadow money can be created.
- Marking collateral to market in a crisis can worsen liquidity spirals, so central banks may need to value collateral at reference prices and backstop markets, which may require rethinking the Bagehot rule.
- A durable arrangement needs coordination between the Treasury and the central bank, described by the authors as a social contract among the state, the central bank and the financial sector.

## Significance

- Gives the vault a theory source for the hierarchy of money, where the test of moneyness is convertibility at par into a higher layer.
- Offers a template for reading stablecoin and tokenised money-market-fund reserves as collateral-dependent private money.
- Links monetary-policy tools (collateral policy, market backstops) to the stability of privately issued money, which bears on central bank responses to stablecoins.
