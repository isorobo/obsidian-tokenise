> **For future Claude.** This note gathers what the vault's sources say about US dollar money held and created outside the United States: the 2008 dollar shortage in global banking, central bank swap lines, offshore credit money in monetary sovereignty theory, dollarisation in emerging markets, cross-border payment rails, and dollar stablecoin demand. As of 27 October 2026 no source note in the vault uses the word "Eurodollar" in its source text; the word appears only in vault metadata and annotations, so the definition below is general-knowledge framing marked [V1]. The only source on dollar funding and swap lines, [[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]], rests on the abstract only.

# Eurodollar (offshore US dollar)

> **Definition.** A Eurodollar is a US dollar deposit (or dollar credit) booked at a bank outside the United States, and so outside US domestic banking regulation, with "Euro" a historical label that does not limit it to Europe [V1].

## Why it matters

The vault's stablecoin material keeps returning to how people and firms outside the United States get access to US dollars, and what that does to their own monetary systems. Before stablecoins, the vault's evidence on that question is a 2009 BIS paper that ties the dollar shortage of the global financial crisis to the structural dollar funding needs of European and Japanese banks, and that singles out central bank swap lines as a policy response ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]], abstract only). A 2022 theory paper says much of today's money is private credit money created offshore in foreign units of account, above all the US dollar ([[Murau-Van-Klooster-Rethinking-Monetary-Sovereignty-2022]]). Recent BIS, IMF and Federal Reserve work treats dollar stablecoins as a new route to dollar access outside the banking system, compared with foreign currency deposits ([[BIS-WP1370-Dollarisation-Monetary-Control-Stablecoins]]). The concept therefore links the [[MOC - Monetary-Hierarchy]] thread on dollarisation and monetary control with the [[MOC - Stablecoins]] material on cross-border payments. The vault itself draws the link between the 2009 paper and stablecoins only in an annotation, which says the paper "Provides the pre-stablecoin baseline for later BIS work on dollar dominance and dollarisation, since stablecoins extend dollar access outside the banking system's balance sheets" ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]], Significance section, vault annotation not source text).

## What the vault's sources say

### Offshore dollar funding and the 2008 dollar shortage

Only one vault source covers this, and the vault note rests on the BIS landing-page abstract and the PDF section outline; the body text was not read ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]).

- McGuire and von Peter (BIS Working Paper 291, 14 October 2009) reconstruct the worldwide consolidated balance sheets of the major national banking systems from the BIS international banking statistics ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]).
- Their finding, as stated in the abstract: "growth (since 2000) in European and Japanese banks' US dollar assets produced structural US dollar funding requirements, setting the stage for the dollar shortage when interbank and swap markets became impaired." ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]).
- Measurement caution: a country's residency-based "national balance sheet" can be a misleading guide to where the vulnerabilities of its banking system lie; the paper uses consolidated balance sheets instead ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]).

### Central bank swap lines

- The same abstract opens: "Among the policy responses to the global financial crisis, the international provision of US dollars via central bank swap lines stands out." ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]).
- The vault note does not name which central banks provided or drew on the swap lines, their size or their terms. That the Federal Reserve was the dollar provider, and how swap lines operate today, are general knowledge here [V2].
- No other vault source note mentions swap lines (search of `10_Sources` and `30_Areas` run 27 October 2026). Gabor and Vestergaard discuss central bank backstops for collateral markets, not swap lines ([[Gabor-Vestergaard-Towards-Theory-Shadow-Money-2016]]).

### Offshore dollar money in monetary sovereignty theory

- Murau and van 't Klooster say public money is a small share of the money supply, that most money is private credit money such as bank deposits and shadow money, and that much of it is created offshore in foreign units of account, above all the US dollar ([[Murau-Van-Klooster-Rethinking-Monetary-Sovereignty-2022]], key claims as summarised in the vault note). The quoted text in the vault note is: "The larger share is made up of private credit money forms such as bank deposits" (Introduction, p. 1320).
- They define effective monetary sovereignty as "the state's ability to use its tools for monetary governance" and criticise the Westphalian view for focusing on public onshore money ([[Murau-Van-Klooster-Rethinking-Monetary-Sovereignty-2022]]).
- For private money, the paper sets out three state responses: ban, accommodate or leave alone; the vault note reads stablecoins as a segment to which these apply ([[Murau-Van-Klooster-Rethinking-Monetary-Sovereignty-2022]], Significance section, vault annotation).
- Gabor and Vestergaard describe money as a hierarchy of promises to pay, credible only if convertible at par into money higher in the hierarchy, and define shadow money as "repo liabilities supported by tradable collateral" ([[Gabor-Vestergaard-Towards-Theory-Shadow-Money-2016]]). The vault note does not record any discussion of offshore dollars or swap lines in that paper.

Both forms share drivers such as exchange rate pass-through and sovereign or banking crises, and both are persistent. "Unlike deposit dollarisation, stablecoin flows seem to be largely unaffected by either broad or specific capital flow restrictions." The vault note calls the findings preliminary. | August 2026 |
| [[IMF-WP-2026-144-Stablecoins-Fixed-Exchange-Rate-Fragility]] (Tan, IMF WP/26/144) | Dollar stablecoins give access to foreign-currency claims outside official allocation channels; their public, high-frequency price can coordinate currency runs when misalignment is severe. ### Cross-border payment rails

- About correspondent banking, the Federal Reserve staff note says: "Over 60 percent of wholesale payments are routed through one or more intermediaries." It also reports that the correspondent network contracted by about 30% over the past decade, and that payment stablecoins could let parties bypass correspondent chains ([[FEDS-Note-2026-03-Payment-Stablecoins-Cross-Border-Payments]], 30 March 2026).
- Du, Huang and Scharfstein find that where stablecoins give better FX pricing on some developed-to-emerging-market corridors, "this advantage reflects distortions associated with capital controls rather than the underlying technology", and that "liquidity between US dollar stablecoins and non-US currencies is generally very thin." ([[BIS-WP1385-Competing-Rails-Crossborder-Payments-Stablecoins]], 19 October 2026; note written from the abstract and findings page).
- A Federal Reserve staff review describes the "stablecoin sandwich" (local currency to dollar stablecoin to destination currency) and expects faster and cheaper transfers, especially for remittances ([[FEDS-Note-2026-08-Decade-US-Cross-Border-Payments-Efforts]], 26 August 2026).
- A Federal Reserve conference summary reports that "Stablecoin-based payment rails can lower the cost of certain cross-border transactions, complementing rather than replacing correspondent banking." ([[FEDS-Note-2026-07-Fifth-Conference-International-Dollar-Stablecoins]], 16 July 2026).
- The FSB says "Global stablecoins are an inherently cross-border and cross-sectoral issue" and points to limits on any single jurisdiction's ability to assess cross-border vulnerabilities; only the FSB overview page was readable ([[FSB-Global-Stablecoin-Arrangements-Cross-Border-Vulnerabilities-2026]], 12 October 2026).

### Dollar stablecoin demand and the international role of the dollar

- Scale: US dollar stablecoin market capitalisation was about $308 billion, up about $71 billion since April 2025, with USDT and USDC holding over 80% of industry assets (as of July 2026, [[NYFed-LSE-Stablecoins-NonCrypto-Shocks-2026-Update]]). Dollar-based stablecoins were about 99% of total stablecoin market value and euro stablecoins under EUR 350 million (as of July 2025, [[ECB-Blog-2025-From-Hype-to-Hazard-Stablecoins-Europe]]).
- Reserve demand: "As of March 2025, stablecoins held over $200 billion in assets, exceeding major foreign investors' short-term US securities holdings." ([[BIS-WP1270-Stablecoins-Safe-Asset-Prices]]).
- Federal Reserve conference view: "Stablecoins are rapidly becoming integrated into the infrastructure of international finance, broadening access to dollar-denominated financial services rather than operating as a separate, self-contained crypto ecosystem." The summary reports Governor Waller and Professor Prasad as arguing that stablecoin growth is more likely to reinforce than erode dollar dominance, and that traditional drivers of dollar demand remain the main explanation for the dollar's international role ([[FEDS-Note-2026-07-Fifth-Conference-International-Dollar-Stablecoins]], conference of 22 and 23 June 2026).
- ECB staff view: "The global market is increasingly dominated by US dollar-based stablecoins." and "If we forgo a common approach, we risk fuelling instability, regulatory arbitrage and global US dollar dominance." ([[ECB-Blog-2025-From-Hype-to-Hazard-Stablecoins-Europe]], 28 July 2025).
- Issuers outside the United States: the vault's summary of the GENIUS Act says foreign stablecoin providers may access US markets if they operate under a Treasury-certified comparable regime, register with the OCC, hold US reserves for customer liquidity and meet AML/CFT and sanctions obligations ([[US-GENIUS-Act-2025-Comprehensive]], Act signed 18 July 2025, vault summary rather than statutory text).

## How the vault's sources use the word

- A search of `10_Sources` and `30_Areas` for "eurodollar" and "euro-dollar" (run 27 October 2026) found the word only in [[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]], and there only as the tag `eurodollar` and in the vault's own Significance annotation ("Background source for a concept note on the Eurodollar market and offshore dollar funding"). The quoted abstract does not use it. No source note quotes a source that uses the word "Eurodollar".
- Outside the source notes, the word appears in the coverage-gap line of [[MOC - Monetary-Hierarchy]] ("The vault holds no note on the Eurodollar market", as at 24 October 2026) and in monitor configuration under `99_Meta`.
- "Offshore" is used in the vault in several unrelated senses: offshore dollar credit money ([[Murau-Van-Klooster-Rethinking-Monetary-Sovereignty-2022]]); offshore destinations for stablecoin transfers in Brazil ([[Ledger-Insights-Brazil-Central-Bank-Stablecoin-Changes]]); offshore tokenised stock products such as [[Ledger-Insights-Coinbase-Tokenised-Stocks-Offshore]]; and a projection in the vault's GENIUS Act analysis that "USDT relegated to offshore trading" ([[US-GENIUS-Act-2025-Comprehensive]], Market Implications section, vault analysis not statutory text). Only the first of these concerns offshore dollar money in the sense of this note.
- "Dollarisation" (or "dollarization") in the vault refers to residents of emerging markets holding dollar claims, through foreign currency deposits or stablecoins ([[BIS-WP1370-Dollarisation-Monetary-Control-Stablecoins]]; [[IMF-Annual-Report-2026-New-Frontiers-Digital-Finance]]). No vault source equates dollar stablecoins with Eurodollars.

## Open points

- **History and size not covered.** The vault holds no source on the history of the Eurodollar market, its size, how offshore dollar deposits are created, or how they relate to US reserve requirements (as at 27 October 2026). The definition above therefore rests on general knowledge [V1].
- **Abstract only.** [[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]] rests on the BIS abstract and PDF section outline; its body text, figures and any discussion of swap line design have not been read into the vault.
- **Swap lines thin.** Swap lines appear in one abstract only. The vault has no Federal Reserve or other central bank source on swap line arrangements, standing facilities or post-2009 use [V2].
- **Stablecoins and offshore dollars.** Whether dollar stablecoins held outside the United States should be analysed as a form of offshore dollar money is not addressed by any vault source. The link exists only in vault annotations ([[BIS-WP291-US-Dollar-Shortage-Global-Banking-Policy-Response]]) and in the comparison with foreign currency deposits ([[BIS-WP1370-Dollarisation-Monetary-Control-Stablecoins]]), which concerns deposit dollarisation, not Eurodollar banking.
- **Different views on dollar dominance.** The Federal Reserve conference summary reports views that stablecoins reinforce dollar dominance ([[FEDS-Note-2026-07-Fifth-Conference-International-Dollar-Stablecoins]]); the ECB staff blog presents dollar stablecoin dominance as a risk for Europe ([[ECB-Blog-2025-From-Hype-to-Hazard-Stablecoins-Europe]]). These are institutional positions from different vantage points, not a direct exchange.
- **Partial readings.** [[BIS-WP1385-Competing-Rails-Crossborder-Payments-Stablecoins]] (abstract and findings page), [[FSB-Global-Stablecoin-Arrangements-Cross-Border-Vulnerabilities-2026]] (overview page) and [[IMF-Speech-2026-08-Georgieva-Financially-More-Fluid-World]] (search-index coverage) were not read in full by the vault.
- **Hierarchy of money.** [[Hierarchy-of-Money]] is linked below but has no concept note yet (as at 27 October 2026).
